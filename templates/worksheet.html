Discussion aid — not a result

Questions to take to your prescriber

This page is preparation for a conversation, not an answer to one. It sets each symptom you entered against each medicine you entered and shows two things: how unspecific the symptom is in this database, and whether the medicine's official label mentions it.

It does not tell you which medicine is causing anything, and it does not put your medicines in any order of suspicion. It cannot: this data has no way to.

Read this first

Do not stop taking a prescribed medicine because of anything on this page — stopping can be more dangerous than continuing. If something here worries you, take the page to your prescriber or pharmacist and ask about it. Do not act on it alone.

This page cannot tell you what is causing your symptoms. Symptoms like tiredness, nausea, aches and dizziness have many causes, most of which are not medicines at all, and only someone who can examine you and knows your history can work out which applies to you. Everything below is a prompt for that conversation.

A reaction appearing in a medicine's label does not mean the medicine is causing yours. A labelled reaction is one that was seen somewhere in trials or in reports — usually in a small minority of people. Most people taking the medicine do not get it.

Questions worth asking

The single most useful thing to bring is a timeline. When did each medicine start or change dose, and when did each symptom begin? That is something a prescriber can work with, and it is something this database knows nothing about.

Other questions worth writing down: is there a non-medicine cause worth ruling out first? Is there a test or a check that would settle it? If a medicine is worth changing, how would we do it and over how long? And if we change nothing, what would make it worth revisiting?

If a label does mention your symptom, a useful way to put it is: "the label for this one mentions {{ blocks[0].term | lower }} — is that worth considering here, or is it unlikely in my case?" That asks for a judgement rather than asserting one.

Notes from the appointment

Where these figures come from

FAERS is a database of voluntary, unvalidated reports. It records reports, not patients, and it contains no record of the people who took a medicine and were fine — so no rate, no probability and no risk can be computed from it by any method. Reported together counts the reports naming both a medicine and a reaction; disproportion bands how much more often that happens than the rest of the database would predict. Neither says the medicine caused anything.

The label column comes from the official product label rather than from FAERS. Label matching is approximate: a reaction recorded as not found may still be described in the label in different words.
