Adverse event signal detection — FDA FAERS

For a given drug, this finds the adverse reactions reported alongside it far more often than the rest of the FDA's adverse event database would predict.

It is a screening instrument. It cannot measure risk, incidence or causation, and it is not a safety assessment of any medicine. What it produces is a ranked list of pairs worth a human looking at.

How a result is produced

For a given drug it finds the adverse events reported alongside it far more often than the rest of the database would predict, then measures the main reasons each result might be misleading: whether the reaction is really the condition the drug treats, whether the reports arrived in one burst, and whether the effect survives comparison against similar drugs rather than against everything.

What it cannot measure

It cannot measure risk. FAERS records reports, not patients, and says nothing about how many people took a drug without incident — so no rate, probability or risk can be computed from it by any method. What comes out is a ranked list of pairs worth a human looking at.

Validated against known answers: statins and rhabdomyolysis reproduce at a reporting odds ratio of 12.96, and cerivastatin — withdrawn worldwide in 2001 for exactly that reaction — ranks highest of any statin without being told.
